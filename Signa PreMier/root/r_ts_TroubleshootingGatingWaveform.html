Troubleshooting the patient monitoring subsystem

Contains instructions for recording physiological data, and examples of common issues.

Overview

This guide contains details on how to record physiological data, as well as a sample set of commonly seen issues that could be encountered in the field. To access the stored data, launch the Physiological Data Tool, click on Load Data, navigate to export > home > service > cclass > Physiological Data Viewer > SampleData > (each folder), and select one file from the desired sample data set.
Note: Files that end with the same timestamp are considered a single physiological data set. For example, in the figure below, all files that end with 0202202218_16_42_775 are from the same sample data set. Selecting any one of these files will load the complete data set.

Recording data

Use the Start and Stop buttons in the Gating Control interface under Record Physiological Waveforms to record the physiological data. The recording is stored in the host computer at export > home > service > log > gating. When Start is clicked, the physiological data will begin recording and will include 30 seconds of data prior to clicking Start. The ongoing recording will be erased if the TPS resets before the recording is stopped. The data is buffered and only saved to disk when either:
  • Stop is clicked
  • 60 minutes recording duration is reached; this is maximum recording duration

Issue 1: AC electrical supply interference

Damaged or improperly installed electrical circuits within or nearby the magnet room, or part of the data acquisition system, can affect the acquired signal of the data acquisition system. In this case, the frequency of the AC electrical supply or a harmonic of it can appear as frequency content in the acquired signals.

In the example below, a large spike at 50Hz can be observed in the ECG2 signal indicating the interference of the AC electrical supply.
Note: The frequency of the AC electrical supply is reported on the MR System Information tab of the Data window, as entry Line/Mains Frequency (Hz).
Data is located at export > home > service > cclass > Physiological Data Viewer > SampleData > 50 Hz Noise. Select the file containing _50Hz_0211202108_31_12_266 in the name.

Issue 2: Broken leads (ECG cable or PAC)

A broken lead is easily detected as a waveform of random noise equaling or nearly equaling the upper and lower limits of the ECG channel (± 4.44 mV). In the example below, the ECG2 time plots show large variation, which is due to the leads not being connected to the patient. This was recorded when the scanner was idle and the patient at the Home position.
Data is located at export > home > service > cclass > Physiological Data Viewer > SampleData > badLead. Select the file containing _lead_0303202209_22_07_891 in the name.
The example below shows the ECG2 channel (top waveform) disconnected from the patient. This could be due to:
  • a bad electrode
  • disconnection from a patient electrode
  • bad ECG cable
  • bad wire in the PAC
Note: The noise in the disconnected state is higher (apparent thicker line) than when connected to the patient.
Data is located at export > home > service > cclass > Physiological Data Viewer > SampleData > badLead. Select the file containing _lead_0203202209_36_01_182 in the name.
The example below shows the result of a disconnected channel (ECG2) during a scan. The interference from the scanner causes the ECG2 waveform to move between the limits of the ECG input channel, and it does not resemble a human ECG waveform shape.
Data is located at export > home > service > cclass > Physiological Data Viewer > SampleData > badLead. Select the file containing _lead_0202202218_16_42_775 in the name.

Issue 3: Scanner noise (gradient)

Gradient-induced scanner noise is generally identified by very short pulses of energy, typically no more than a few milliseconds in duration. The example below shows the beginning of a scan where there is a very strong crusher pulse followed by the slice select pulses. In most cases, the system can identify these sharp peaks and ignore them when calculating triggers. However, in this case, the lead-off crusher is so powerful that it caused the trigger (green circle) to be missed.

Missed triggers like this on ECG2 at 15:38:24.8 are called false negative triggers. A false positive trigger can be seen on ECG3 at 15:38:28.4, where the system erroneously issued a trigger mark due to the scanner interference. Both types of false triggers should be investigated, but scanner noise of this amplitude is normal. Scanner noise that is near the upper and lower limits of the ECG channel suggests a system problem.
Data is located at export > home > service > cclass > Physiological Data Viewer > SampleData > scannerNoise. Select the file containing _noise_0107202215_38_38_535 in the name.

Issue 4: Scanner noise (DD boards on body coil)

The example below shows a patient with an irregular heartbeat being scanned on a 1.5T wide bore system. The scan starts at 08:03:34. These pulses are indicative of the high voltage used by dynamic disable (DD) to turn the body coil on and off. Newer body coils for 1.5T wide bore (including and after January 2022) do not exhibit this interference. No 3.0T scanners exhibit this type of interference under normal conditions. The level of this interference is moderate. On large patients, the interference can be up to the full range of the ECG channel; in this case the interference results in many false positive triggers, which renders the ECG gating unusable for this patient. The customer’s only recourse is to switch to PPG gating.
Data is located at export > home > service > cclass > Physiological Data Viewer > SampleData > scannerNoise. Select the file containing _ddbd_0202202208_05_30_451 in the name.
An extreme case of DD interference is shown below. This suggests that the scanner should have the body coil updated.
Data is located at export > home > service > cclass > Physiological Data Viewer > SampleData > scannerNoise. Select the file containing _noise_1119202115_12_09_421 in the name.

Issue 5: Base noise level

The example below shows the standard deviation (STD) of the noise in the disconnected ECG2 channel being above 100 microvolts. A terminated ECG cable (where the two electrodes from a white or black pair are connected together, such as with an MR safe M4 screw) normally exhibits less than 15 microvolts.
Data is located at export > home > service > cclass > Physiological Data Viewer > SampleData > baseNoise. Select the file containing _noise_0203202209_53_38_182 in the name.
The example below illustrates a good terminated noise case where the noise STD is less than 15 microvolts. The ECG leads can be easily terminated together using an MR safe M4 screw (stainless steel or brass) at least 20 mm long.
Data is located at export > home > service > cclass > Physiological Data Viewer > SampleData > goodData. Select the file containing _good_0210202008_26_04_997 in the name.
